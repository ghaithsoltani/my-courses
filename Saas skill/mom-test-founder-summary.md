# The Mom Test — Founder's Working Summary

*Written for a solo developer in Tunisia building a SaaS that reduces failed deliveries for e-commerce sellers. Paraphrased and reorganized by relevance, not by chapter order.*

---

## 1. The book in 5 lines

1. Most founders learn the wrong things from customer conversations because they ask people to judge their idea, and people are polite.
2. The fix is to stop pitching and start asking about the other person's real life: what they did, what it cost, what they tried.
3. Good conversations give you **facts** (what happens), **commitments** (what they give up), and **advancement** (the next real step toward buying). Compliments are noise.
4. You are not asking customers what to build. You are collecting facts about their problem, then deciding the solution yourself.
5. It is a validation book only. It does **not** teach building, deploying, pricing models, or growth. Use it for the first 4–8 weeks, then move to the next resource.

## 2. Key lessons, ranked by importance for you

### Lesson 1: Never ask "Is this a good idea?" or "Would you buy this?"
People say yes to be kind. Opinions about your idea and promises about the future are worth nothing.
**Example for you:** "Would you pay for a tool that stops refused deliveries?" → everybody says yes. Useless.
**Instead:** "Tell me about the last order that came back. What happened?"

### Lesson 2: The three rules (memorize these)
1. Talk about **their life**, not your idea.
2. Ask about **specific past events**, not generic habits or future opinions.
3. **Talk less, listen more.**

If they don't know you have an idea, they can't lie about it.

### Lesson 3: Three kinds of bad data. Spot them and recover.
| Bad data | What it sounds like | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| **Compliments** | "Great idea!", "Keep me posted" | Thank them, then deflect and go back to facts |
| **Fluff** (generics, future, hypotheticals) | "I usually…", "I would definitely…", "I might…" | **Anchor** it: "When was the last time?" "Walk me through it." |
| **Ideas / feature requests** | "You should add X" | **Dig:** "Why do you want that? What would it let you do?" Understand it, don't obey it |

If you leave a meeting saying "it went really well" without a specific fact or commitment, you probably collected compliments.

### Lesson 4: Commitment is the only real proof
A conversation only counts as successful if the person gave up something valuable or moved forward. Three currencies:
- **Time:** a follow-up meeting, a trial with real orders, sharing their workflow.
- **Reputation:** introducing you to a partner, a team member, or the person who controls the money.
- **Money:** a deposit, a pre-payment, a signed letter of intent.

A friendly "let me know when it's ready" is a polite no. Ask for something concrete instead.

### Lesson 5: Ask the scary question
Each conversation should include at least one question that could kill your idea. Imagine the startup failed; why? Imagine it succeeded; what had to be true? Test those points first.

### Lesson 6: Lukewarm answers are valuable
"Hmm, not sure" or "that's neat" means they don't care. That's reliable information. Don't pitch harder to convert them; learn why they don't care, thank them, leave.

### Lesson 7: Start broad, then zoom in (avoid the "premature zoom")
Don't start with "what's your biggest problem with failed deliveries?" if you haven't confirmed it matters to them. Start with "What are the biggest headaches in running your shop?" If failed deliveries come up on their own, you have a real signal. Then zoom in.

### Lesson 8: Choose a narrow, findable customer segment
If your feedback is all over the place, your segment is too broad. A good segment is a **who + where pair**: you know exactly who they are and where to find them. Keep slicing until you do.

### Lesson 9: Separate market risk from product risk
- **Market risk:** do they have the problem, will they pay, are there enough of them?
- **Product risk:** can *I* actually build it and make it work?
Conversations can prove market risk. For heavy product risk, you must build earlier and learn from real usage.

### Lesson 10: Your first customers are emotional ones
The best early customers have the problem, know they have it, have budget, and have already built an ugly workaround. When someone reacts with real frustration, dig and keep them close. These are your first sales.

### Lesson 11: Keep it casual, not an "interview"
Short chats beat formal meetings early on. Formal interviews make people stiff and polite. The early learning (does the problem exist, how do they cope) takes 5–15 minutes.

### Lesson 12: Don't be the only brain holding the learning
Write notes, review them with someone, share exact quotes. (You're solo, so see Section 7.)

## 3. Action plan: step by step

**Step 1: Choose a narrow segment (1 day)**
Write down who exactly, and where you'll find them (see Section 7).

**Step 2: Write your "list of 3" (1 hour)**
The 3 most important things you need to learn. Rewrite after every few conversations.

**Step 3: Write your risk list (30 min)**
"If this startup fails, why?" and "What must be true for it to win?" Turn the scariest items into questions.

**Step 4: Find 10–15 people to talk to (week 1)**
Start with anyone you already know, then ask each person for an intro to others.

**Step 5: Have conversations, keep them short and casual**
Use the question bank in Section 4. Take notes during or immediately after.

**Step 6: End every conversation with the right closing questions**
- "Who else should I talk to?"
- "Is there anything I should have asked?"
- A concrete next step or commitment (when relevant).

**Step 7: Review and update (after every 3–5 conversations)**
What did you learn? Are the answers consistent? If they're all over the map, narrow the segment.

**Step 8: Stop when you stop hearing new things**
Usually 3–5 conversations for a simple, focused segment. If you've had 10+ and answers still conflict, the segment is too broad.

**Step 9: Move from learning to commitment**
When the problem is confirmed, show a simple prototype or even a manual service, and ask for a commitment (pilot, data, deposit).

**Step 10: Decide: go / change segment / change problem / stop**
Bad news early is cheap. It is a win.

## 4. Frameworks and templates, ready to use

### 4.1 Question bank (pass the Mom Test)

**Opening (broad, to see if it matters at all)**
- "What are the biggest headaches in running your online shop right now?"
- "What are you spending the most time and money fixing at the moment?"

**Anchoring to the past**
- "Tell me about the last order that was refused or never answered. What happened, step by step?"
- "When was the last time that happened?"
- "What did you do about it?"

**Cost and importance**
- "What did that cost you? (shipping both ways, packaging, ad spend, time)"
- "What are the implications when it happens? How often per week?"
- "Is this in your top 3 problems right now?"

**Current solutions and workarounds**
- "How do you handle it today?" / "What else have you tried?"
- "Who does that: you, an employee, the courier?"
- "What do you love or hate about that approach?"
- "What would it cost you to switch?"

**Money and decisions (B2B)**
- "Where does the budget for that come from?"
- "Who else decides when you pick a tool or service?"

**Digging**
- "Why do you bother with it that way?"
- "That seems to really bother you. Tell me more."
- "Why haven't you fixed this already?"

**Closing**
- "Who else should I talk to?"
- "Is there anything else I should have asked?"

### 4.2 Fix-it phrases
- **Deflect a compliment:** "Thanks. Before I forget: how do you handle this today?"
- **Recover from pitching:** "Sorry, I slipped into pitch mode. Back to what you were saying about…"
- **Anchor fluff:** "When was the last time that happened? Can you walk me through it?"
- **Dig into a feature request:** "What would that let you do?" / "How are you coping without it?"
- **Push for a decision:** "Why not set it up now?" (if they claim a problem but never tried a fix)

### 4.3 Meeting-request template (Vision / Framing / Weakness / Pedestal / Ask)

> **Vision:** I'm working on reducing the losses online sellers face from failed and refused deliveries.
> **Framing:** I'm at an early stage and have nothing to sell. I just want to understand how it really works.
> **Weakness:** I haven't run a shop myself, so I'm missing the day-to-day reality of handling returns.
> **Pedestal:** You've been selling online for a while and handle deliveries every day.
> **Ask:** Could I ask you a few questions for 15 minutes this week?

Translate it into Arabic, French, or Tunisian Arabic as the person prefers. Keep it short.

### 4.4 Commitment ladder (what to ask for, from weakest to strongest)
1. A second conversation with a clear goal
2. Sharing a sample of real order data (anonymized)
3. An intro to a courier partner or to the person who controls the budget
4. A pilot on real orders for a fixed period
5. A public testimonial / case study
6. A deposit, pre-payment, or signed letter of intent

### 4.5 Reading answers

| Answer | Reading |
|---|---|
| "I love it!" | Compliment. No data. |
| "Let me know when it launches." | Polite no. Ask for something today. |
| "I'd definitely use it." | Fluff. Ask for a pilot or deposit. |
| "When can we start?" | Possible yes. Check what they're giving up. |
| "Can I buy the prototype?" | Strong yes. |
| "Let me introduce you to my courier / partner." | Good. Make it specific. |
| "Hmm, not sure." | Lukewarm = they don't care. Learn why and move on. |

### 4.6 Note-taking shorthand (one card or row per fact)
- `:)` excited, `:(` angry, `:|` embarrassed
- `PAIN` problem, `GOAL` what they're trying to achieve
- `OBSTACLE` what blocks them, `WORKAROUND` how they cope
- `CONTEXT` background
- `FEATURE` request or buying criteria
- `$` money, budget, buying process
- `NAME` person or company mentioned
- `TODO` follow-up promised

Use a spreadsheet: one row per fact, columns for date, person, signal type, exact quote. Sort by signal type later.

### 4.7 Pricing signals from the book
- Don't ask "how much would you pay?". Ask what the problem costs them today and what they currently pay for any workaround (staff time, tools, lost orders).
- The workaround cost is your price anchor.
- A deposit or pre-payment is the best proof of price.

### 4.8 Customer slicing (to narrow your segment)
Ask yourself repeatedly:
- Within this group, who wants it most?
- Why do they want it?
- Who else has the same motivation?
- What are they already doing about it? Where can I find them?
Pick the group that is **profitable, easy to reach, and rewarding** for you to work with.

## 5. Common mistakes the book warns about

1. **Fishing for compliments** ("I have an idea, do you like it?").
2. **Exposing your ego** so people protect your feelings instead of telling the truth.
3. **Being pitchy** and taking over the conversation.
4. **Being too formal** (interview tone, rating scales, calendar blocks for every chat).
5. **Collecting compliments instead of facts and commitments** ("everyone loves it").
6. **Zooming in too early** on a problem you haven't confirmed matters.
7. **Talking to the wrong people**: too broad a segment, missing a stakeholder, or only talking to impressive senior people instead of real users.
8. **Obeying every feature request** instead of understanding the reason behind it.
9. **Zombie leads:** people who stay friendly but never commit to anything.
10. **Becoming a learning bottleneck:** keeping insights in your head and ignoring notes.
11. **Not asking the scary question** out of fear.
12. **Going through the motions** (see the checklist below).

**Signs you're just going through the motions**
- You're talking more than they are.
- You got compliments instead of facts.
- You don't know what happens next.
- You have no notes.
- An unexpected answer didn't change your plan.
- You weren't afraid of any question you asked.

## 6. What you can skip, and why

| Book content | Why you can skip or adapt |
|---|---|
| Advice on investors, advisory boards, university professors for intros | You're not raising money yet. Use your own network and communities first. |
| Organizing meetups, conference talks, industry events | Too heavy for now. A small Facebook/WhatsApp community post is the equivalent. |
| Strong preference for in-person over calls | Good ideal, but in your context WhatsApp voice or calls are often the realistic option. Use them, but keep them casual and start with a real story, not a script. |
| Team review with cofounders | You're solo. Use a substitute (see Section 7). |
| Steve Blank's 3-meeting structure | Understand the idea, but the book itself favors quick casual chats. |
| Enterprise-sales details (multiple stakeholders, long cycles) | Your buyers are small sellers; the owner usually decides. Keep it simple unless you target larger merchants later. |
| Hiring someone to run the conversations | The book says don't. Founders must hear it themselves. Agree. |

**What the book doesn't cover (find these elsewhere):**
- How to build the MVP, pick tech, deploy
- Pricing models and billing
- Onboarding, retention, churn
- Marketing and growth channels
- Legal and payments setup

## 7. Applied to your startup

### 7.1 Your idea in the book's language
- **Problem hypothesis:** online sellers lose money when delivered orders are refused or the buyer doesn't pick up the phone.
- **Customer hypothesis:** small to mid-size online sellers who ship with cash on delivery (assumption to test).
- **Risks to test:**
  - **Market risk:** Is this a top-3 pain? How much does each failed delivery cost them? Are they paying for any workaround today? Will they pay a monthly fee?
  - **Product risk (the elephant):** Can you actually reduce refusals? (For example: pre-delivery confirmation, flagging risky orders, tracking repeat refusers.) If your product doesn't reduce losses, the pain doesn't matter.
  - **Partner risk:** couriers. Will they share status data, accept integration, or does the shop control that relationship? Understand their constraints as well as the merchants'.

### 7.2 Your "list of 3" for merchants (starting version)
1. How often do deliveries fail or get refused, and what does each one actually cost them?
2. What do they already do about it (calls, messages, blacklists, courier rules) and how much time or money does it take?
3. Who decides to adopt a new tool, and what would make them try it with real orders?

*(Rewrite after 3–5 conversations.)*

### 7.3 Narrow the segment (a first slice; adjust after talking)
Don't start with "all e-commerce". Candidate who-where pairs to test:
- Sellers who run shops on social media with cash-on-delivery orders and handle a meaningful daily order volume
- Sellers who already pay staff to confirm orders by phone
- Sellers who've complained about courier returns in seller groups

Where to find them: Tunisian e-commerce and dropshipping groups on Facebook, seller communities, courier pickup points, shops you can order from yourself and ask about returns. Pick the slice that's easiest for you to reach and most clearly in pain.

### 7.4 Questions to avoid for your idea
- ❌ "Would you use a tool that predicts refused orders?"
- ❌ "How much would you pay per month?"
- ❌ "Do you think this would help your business?"
- ✅ "Tell me about the last refused order. What happened?"
- ✅ "How many came back last month? What did that cost you?"
- ✅ "What do you do before shipping to avoid it?"

### 7.5 You're solo: adjustments
- Record the conversation (ask permission) or write notes right after, so nothing sits only in your head.
- Review notes weekly yourself, and share a summary with a friend, mentor, or classmate who will challenge you. Ask them: "What did I miss? What am I assuming?"
- Because you're technical, you'll want to code early. The book's point: coding before confirming the problem is the expensive mistake. Prototype manually first if you can (for example, call or message buyers for a few sellers and measure whether refusals drop).

### 7.6 Tunisia / international notes
- Keep conversations in the customer's preferred language (Derja, French, Arabic).
- When you expand to other countries, repeat the process: the "who + where" and the workaround will differ per market. Don't assume Tunisian findings transfer.

### 7.7 One first step for this week
**Do this within 7 days:**
1. Write your segment guess (one sentence) and your list of 3.
2. List 20 people you could talk to (people you know, shops you've bought from, seller groups).
3. Send the meeting-request template (Section 4.3) to the first 5.
4. Hold at least 2 casual conversations of 10–15 minutes, taking notes using Section 4.6.

**Success criterion:** you leave with at least one concrete fact about what a failed delivery costs and how the seller handles it today, and one real next step (an intro, shared data, or a pilot).

## 8. Quick checklist before any conversation

- [ ] I know the 3 things I want to learn.
- [ ] I know which commitment or next step I'll ask for, if relevant.
- [ ] I will not mention my idea unless I need to.
- [ ] I will ask about specific past events.
- [ ] I will ask at least one question that scares me.
- [ ] I will take notes and review them within 24 hours.
